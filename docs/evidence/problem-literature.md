## Summary

Review type: rapid scoping review (not systematic). The original search ran on 2 October 2026 through Consensus (nine queries). Sixteen peer-reviewed sources were retained, and each was rechecked independently on the same day.

### (a) What goes wrong on arrival

Acculturative stress is the best-quantified problem. A 2024 systematic review of 29 studies, of which 26 entered the meta-analysis (total N = 7,247), found a pooled correlation of r = 0.39 between acculturative stress and psychological outcomes overall, and r = 0.41 with depression [1]. Fifteen of those studies were conducted in the United States and four in China, so the estimate is weighted toward those settings [1]. Two reviews of adjustment predictors, both limited to students in the United States, point to the same protective factors. A review of 64 studies published between 1990 and 2009 reported stress, social support, and English language proficiency among the most frequently reported predictors of psychosocial adjustment [2]. A later review of 30 quantitative studies of undergraduate international students found acculturative stress, psychological adjustment, social belonging, depression, and anxiety to be the most commonly researched outcomes [3].

The burden starts before the flight. A literature review of challenges from pre-departure to post-study lists obtaining accurate information, the admission procedure, and preparing visa documents as common pre-departure difficulties, followed by language, financial challenges, and cultural adjustment in the host country [4]. This supports the product's scope of starting at the admission letter and its document-check step. Loneliness is a separate, recurring theme: the authors of a pilot at the University of Padua describe it as prevalent among international students and often worsened by cultural and linguistic barriers, although they give no prevalence rate [9].

### (b) Peer mentoring, buddy programs, and pre-arrival support

The buddy-match feature has the strongest intervention evidence. In Canada, newcomer international students paired with Canadian student mentors met weekly throughout a semester; participants showed positive changes in sociocultural and psychological adaptation and a reduction in acculturative stress, and at the end of the program they had higher psychological adaptation and lower acculturative stress than control participants who had not taken part [7]. A larger quasi-experimental field study in Australia (43 local mentors, 126 international mentees, 238 controls, 18 to 19 weeks) reported effects in intercultural communication, cultural adjustment, life skills, and well-being [8].

The Padua pilot adds a design hint, but it is weak evidence. Of 49 students recruited, only 22 completed the 8-week study (55.10% did not), in groups of five or six. Interventions with in-person activities had significant advantages over self-help materials alone for interaction improvement and number of relationships [9]. The blended and peer-to-peer groups also reported lower loneliness, anxiety, and depression scores than controls; however, the authors state that the statistical tests on these standardized questionnaires did not reveal significant results [9]. In practice, this suggests that the app should route students to a real person, because digital self-help alone was the weakest condition, but the pilot does not prove a mental health effect.

For pre-arrival support, 31 semi-structured interviews with postgraduate international students at a Malaysian research university identified a Student Ambassador Program, information from friends and colleagues, and academics building rapport before arrival as the pre-arrival assistance received. The author reports that this had a positive impact on students' engagement, satisfaction, motivation, and sense of belonging [10]. Because the study is qualitative, the finding is self-reported and is not a measured effect size.

### (c) UAE and Gulf evidence

Local evidence exists but is thin and qualitative. One study at an unnamed multicultural institution in the United Arab Emirates (first author at Al Ain University, second author at the University of Liverpool) ran six focus groups with 40 first-year international students of ten nationalities. International students made up 67.92% of the institution's 4,720 undergraduates in academic year 2017-2018 and represented more than 70 nationalities [5]. Freshmen orientation was one of three themes shaping the transition, alongside academic experiences and the institutional environment. Most participants argued against holding orientation in the second week of the semester and said that information in the first week, or before they arrived, would have helped them prepare [5]. One participant said, "There was no orientation about the socio-cultural boundaries here. My friends who live with me in the dormitory told me about them" [5]. The authors also note that delays in obtaining visas or travel documents can delay arrival, and that for this reason some institutions, including the one studied, have moved orientation to the second week [5].

A survey of 152 freshmen at an American university in the UAE found that many rely heavily on the support of peers, especially co-nationals. However, that sample had completed high school in the UAE, so it describes the transition to university and not arrival in the country [6]. Both findings fit the product: orientation content should be available on demand before and independent of the arrival date, and matching by nationality or language reflects how students already seek help.

### (d) AI chatbots for student support and newcomer onboarding

Evidence here is early-stage: design studies, pilots, and operational deployments at single institutions. Politecnico di Torino built chatbots for the help desk of its Recruitment and Admissions Unit after receiving more than 20,000 requests from more than 100 countries since January 2024. An intent-based approach performed best on frequently asked questions and well-defined tickets, and a generative-only approach suited open-ended requests [11]. A human-in-the-loop large language model (LLM) system at one university was designed, deployed, and evaluated using a dataset of 135,359 real student and staff interactions collected over 15 years. It reported 95.88% classification accuracy in evaluation (82.21% staff acceptance in practice), 94.81% of AI-generated draft responses adopted with minor edits, a 30.8% reduction in resolution time, a 32.6% decrease in misrouting, and user satisfaction rising from 3.6 to 4.9 out of 5 [14]. A chatbot for first-line communication between student services and students during COVID-19, motivated by isolated students living away from their families, especially international students, was tested by more than 160 students and student-services staff [15].

For newcomers generally, RefuGPT combined GPT-4 with retrieval-augmented generation over official and community-based information and was evaluated in a naturalistic setting with Ukrainian refugees in Switzerland. The authors attribute refugee disorientation to language barriers, a digital divide, high urgency, and an evolving situation, and they describe a move from information-seeking to information-asking [12]. An earlier co-design study with migrants starts from the premise that searching information portals can be exhausting and involves time-consuming information overload; this is the paper's stated motivation and not one of its measured results [13]. The architecture implication is direct: ground answers in retrieved official sources and keep a human escalation path.

### Honest limits

A 2026 scoping review of LLM tools for the psychological support of university students (18 studies, concentrated in East Asia) names international students as an underrepresented research population and lists linguistic and cultural barriers and unresolved ethical concerns about crisis management among the challenges [16]. No study found evaluates an end-to-end AI arrival companion with document checking for international students, and no quantitative UAE study measures administrative burden on arrival. The pitch should therefore claim that each component is evidence-informed, and it should not claim that the combined product is proven.

## Open gaps

- Verification depth still differs by source. Full text was read only for [5]; [1], [7], and [9] were checked on the publisher page; the rest were checked at abstract level. Figures from [8], [11], [12], [13], [14], [15], and [16] are abstract-level and were not rechecked against the full text.
- The publisher abstract of [2] (Elsevier) returned HTTP 403. Its 64-study count and predictor list come from secondary descriptions in two peer-reviewed papers. If the report quotes [2], it should cite the number cautiously or omit it.
- The DOI for [5] is unreliable (10.32674/jis.v14i1.4967 resolves to an issue page; Crossref holds a truncated DOI). Cite the journal URL and volume 13, issue 3, pp. 323-341.
- Reference [10] carries two dates: published online 2 February 2021 and in print in 2023 (vol. 27, no. 8). Choose one convention for the IEEE reference list.
- Sample sizes for the mentoring study [7] are not in the abstract and were not retrieved.
- The mental health benefit in [9] is not statistically significant and rests on 22 completers. The report must not present it as a proven effect.
- Reviews [2] and [3] cover students in the United States only, and the meta-analysis [1] is dominated by United States samples. None includes UAE data.
- No peer-reviewed quantitative study was found that measures administrative burden, visa or Emirates ID processing friction, or time-to-settle for international students in the UAE or Abu Dhabi. The only UAE arrival evidence is one qualitative study at a single institution [5]; [6] covers students already resident in the UAE.
- No study was found that evaluates an end-to-end AI arrival companion for international students, or vision-based document checking for student or migrant onboarding. Chatbot evidence is limited to design-science studies, pilots, and single-institution deployments; none is a randomized trial in this population.
- No source was found on voice or multilingual (Russian, Turkmen, Arabic) conversational support for newcomers, so that feature rests on the general language-barrier evidence in [2], [4], and [12] only.
- "Administrative burden" and "information overload" were not found measured as named constructs in international-student samples. The support is indirect: [4] on information and visa-document difficulty, and [13], where information overload is a stated premise.
- No prevalence figure for loneliness among international students was verified; the report should not quote a percentage.
- This is a rapid scoping review, not a systematic one. The original search returned three results per Consensus query; PubMed and Google Scholar were not searched.
- Smith and Khawaja (2011), International Journal of Intercultural Relations 35, pp. 699-713, and Fu et al. (2024) were not verified and carry no claims here.
